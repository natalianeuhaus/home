Greetings from Niagara

A research-driven photography and multimedia project tracing the human and environmental consequences of Manhattan Project and Cold War industry across Western New York.

I

The waterfalls + industry

Driving through America’s Honeymoon Capital, Niagara Falls, one might hear the roar of the waterfalls, but this roar can’t silence the echoes of a buried secret: this city is heavily radioactive. Niagara Falls’s involvement in the Manhattan Project and the nuclear buildup that followed during the Cold War left a radioactive scar on the region. For thousands of years, this area will face the consequences of this contamination.

The origin of this American tragedy is rooted in what attracts tourists year after year: its waterfalls. Niagara Falls has long been a source of cheap and abundant hydroelectric power. The city’s industrialization began in the 1850s, when water-powered mills and factories were built beside the Falls. Then, in 1895, the Adams Power Station became the world’s first large-scale alternating-current power plant. Using Nikola Tesla’s AC system, it supplied Niagara Falls’s factories with cheap, abundant, and reliable electricity.

I

Electrochemistry + electrometallurgy

This new supply of electricity opened a new era of metal production and electrochemical discovery. Electric furnaces could now reach temperatures exceeding 7,000 degrees Fahrenheit, enabling chemical changes in materials that had previously been impossible. By 1950, Niagara Falls was one of the nation’s leading industrial centers, with more than 90,000 residents. These were not ordinary factories. Hooker Electrochemical became a major producer of chlorine and caustic soda, chemicals used in water treatment, paper, plastics, solvents, and wartime explosives. Linde produced industrial gases. Electromet manufactured specialized alloys and became the nation’s largest producer of uranium metal. Vanadium produced metals used to strengthen steel. Carborundum pioneered silicon carbide, the synthetic abrasive that transformed industrial grinding and cutting. Together, these factories placed Niagara Falls at the forefront of chemistry, metallurgy, and nuclear production.

Their names later changed, but many remain recognizable. Hooker was acquired by Occidental Petroleum and became Occidental Chemical, or OxyChem. Union Carbide, which controlled Linde, Electromet, and Vanadium, is now owned by Dow. Linde was spun off as Praxair and later became part of today’s Linde. Carborundum survives as a Saint-Gobain brand.

II

Atomic Niagara

Hooker Electrochemical Company, Union Carbide and its subsidiaries Linde and Electromet, Carborundum, and other regional companies became part of a wartime production network built around uranium. Factories refined ore, converted uranium compounds into metal, recovered uranium from waste, fabricated rods and components, and experimented with processes designed to maximize yield.

General Leslie Groves, who directed the Manhattan Project, cultivated relationships with industrial operators whose existing laboratories, power supply, rail access, and culture of secrecy made Western New York particularly useful. That secrecy also meant that generations of workers handled radioactive materials without a public understanding of the risks or the full scope of the work.
III

LOOW / NFSS

Much of the radioactive waste generated by the regional uranium program was transported to the Lake Ontario Ordnance Works, or LOOW, a vast federal reservation near Lewiston. Built during World War II, the site was used for storage, handling, burial, repackaging, and disposal of radioactive residues and contaminated equipment from Manhattan Project and Atomic Energy Commission operations.

The site later became the Niagara Falls Storage Site. In 1986, an interim waste containment structure was built to consolidate a large volume of radioactive material. The word interim matters: the storage site remains part of an unfinished federal cleanup story, surrounded by communities, farms, roads, and schools.

IV

Sludge + slag

The waste did not remain neatly contained inside federal storage or factory boundaries. A New York State Assembly investigation following Love Canal documented millions of gallons of radioactively contaminated chemical waste entering wells, sewers, and drainage systems connected to the region’s waterways.

Solid radioactive residues created another pathway. Slag and contaminated fill accumulated at factories and storage areas. Historical testimony and later investigations describe workers taking material home for driveways and foundations, while trucking and construction practices allowed similar material to move through the region as inexpensive fill.

V

The uranium chain

The material moved through a chain of specialized facilities. Linde in Tonawanda refined uranium ore into uranium compounds including uranium tetrafluoride, the green powder commonly called “green salt.” Electromet in Niagara Falls converted that material into uranium metal, producing radioactive C-2 slag as a byproduct. Hooker Chemical treated slag to recover uranium that remained in the waste.

Other facilities carried the process forward. Regional steel plants rolled and fabricated uranium components, while Carborundum participated in uranium-related research and later nuclear-fuel development. Bell Aerospace’s weapons work overlapped geographically with this expanding network of nuclear-material research.

This is why the project does not treat a single factory or dump as the whole story. Each process created a different waste stream, and those waste streams moved through shared rail lines, trucking routes, sewers, waterways, storage areas, landfills, and neighborhoods.

VI

The surveys

In 1979, Oak Ridge National Laboratory conducted an aerial radiological survey of Niagara County and identified numerous areas of elevated radiation. A later mobile survey in the mid-1980s investigated locations across Niagara Falls and documented more than one hundred elevated gamma-radiation locations.

The limits of those surveys became clearer over time. New radiological findings continued to appear during road work, construction, and site investigations, including locations near the Robert Moses Parkway, the Whirlpool Bridge area, Niagara Falls State Park, and properties on Main Street.

The pattern raised a central question that drives this project: if radioactive industrial material was repeatedly moved and reused as fill, how much of the city was never comprehensively surveyed?

VII

FUSRAP + Love Canal

By the 1970s, the federal government had begun identifying former Manhattan Project and Atomic Energy Commission sites that required radiological investigation and cleanup. That work became the Formerly Utilized Sites Remedial Action Program, or FUSRAP. Western New York contains an unusually dense concentration of sites connected to this history.

At almost the same moment, Love Canal forced the country to confront what industrial disposal could mean for a residential community. Residents organized around illnesses, miscarriages, birth defects, and chemicals surfacing around homes and a school built beside a former industrial dump. Their campaign helped propel the creation of the federal Superfund program.

VIII

Decades of cancer

For thousands of years, this area will face the consequences of radioactive contamination. No other area in the world, not even Chornobyl’s exclusion zone, is comparable. It’s no accident that cancer rates in this area of western New York have remained disturbingly high for the past 40 years and are the highest in the U.S.

XIII

The Federal Connection

After Love Canal, the New York State Assembly created a special task force to investigate claims that military and federal activities were connected to waste disposal in Niagara Falls. The resulting 293-page report, The Federal Connection, documented relationships among the U.S. Army, the Manhattan Engineer District, federal contractors, and the region’s chemical and metallurgical industries.

Love Canal and federal involvement were merely the proverbial tip of the iceberg.

The Federal Connection Report, page 20

Greetings from Niagara builds on that investigation with records that were unavailable or difficult to access when the state report was produced: worker-dose reconstruction files, FUSRAP records, environmental investigations, archival footage, historical surveys, court and legislative records, and hundreds of government documents scattered across multiple agencies.

The project follows those fragments across factories, roads, waterways, homes, public spaces, and the people who have lived with the consequences. It is an attempt to reconstruct a regional history that was divided among agencies and hidden behind industrial and military secrecy.

What follows is the unraveling of a national story with consequences that remain embedded in the landscape of Western New York.
