Niagara and Erie Counties

Radioactive waste did not move by one route.

It left factories as solid residues and liquid effluent, entered storage and disposal sites, and moved through systems that were fragmented, poorly documented, and difficult to reconstruct.

Production, storage, and disposal

A regional network—not a single source.

Linde, Electromet, Hooker, Carborundum, LOOW, and Union Carbide’s company landfill are known points in that network. They are not proven origins for every later finding.

Solid waste

Slag and contaminated fill moved outward.

Radioactive material was later found beneath roads, driveways, parking lots, and properties across parts of Niagara and Erie Counties. In many places, the surviving record cannot identify one source.

Liquid waste

Other wastes moved through water and underground.

Records describe liquid and process wastes entering disposal wells, sewers, creeks, and rivers—pathways that could carry contamination beyond the place where it was produced.

November 4, 2017

New York required radiation screening at covered landfills.

Landfills accepting municipal solid waste or drilling and production waste—public or private—must monitor incoming loads with fixed radiation detectors.

The present-day gap

Not every movement of fill passes through a detector.

Construction debris, excavated material, slag, and reused fill may move through other pathways. New York’s routine fill analysis lists chemical contaminants and asbestos, but not radionuclides.

The pathway has not necessarily disappeared

Radioactive slag can be excavated, transported, and reused again.

If the material never passes through a radiation detector, its radioactivity may remain invisible.
